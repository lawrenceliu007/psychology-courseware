CIE Psychology 9990 — Exam Practice Handbook

Based on real exam questions from all 12 Core Studies. Built from actual past-paper patterns, not generic advice.

1. Exam Overview

Paper Structure

CIE Psychology 9990 Paper 1 tests your knowledge of 12 Core Studies. Questions range from 1-mark recall to 12-mark extended evaluation. Understanding which type of question you're answering is the single most important skill — because the type determines your answer structure.

Mark RangeQuestion TypeAOWhat Examiners WantFramework?
1 markState / Define / IdentifyAO1Precise factual recall — one correct answerNo
2 marksOutline / State + ExplainAO1Brief factual answer, sometimes two partsNo
3–4 marksDescribe / Explain / SuggestAO1/AO2Structured recall or application to a scenarioLight
6 marksEvaluate / DiscussAO32–3 evaluation points with evidenceYes
8–12 marksAssess / To what extent / Evaluate (extended)AO3Balanced evaluation + conclusionYes (full)
Golden Rule: The number of marks = the number of distinct, developed points you need to make. For a 10-mark evaluation, you need about 4 developed points (2 strengths + 2 weaknesses) plus a brief conclusion. For a 2-mark question, write 2 sentences maximum.

2. Question Type Taxonomy (with real exam examples)

2.1 State / Define / Identify 1–2 marks AO1

These questions test pure recall. You either know the answer or you don't. No framework needed — just write the fact.

Real Exam Examples
  • State the type of problem that Biyu is concerned about. [1] Hölzel
  • Define what is meant by operant conditioning. [1] Saavedra
  • State what is meant by the term 'field experiment'. [2] Piliavin
  • What is meant by the term 'theory of mind'? [2] Baron-Cohen
Model Answer (1 mark)

Demand characteristics.

Model Answer (2 marks)

A field experiment is a study conducted in a natural real-world setting where the researcher manipulates the independent variable, but extraneous variables are harder to control than in a laboratory.

Common mistake: Writing a full paragraph for a 1-mark question. One word or one sentence is enough. Examiners award the mark for the key term — extra words waste time.

2.2 Outline 2–4 marks AO1

Brief, factual description. No evaluation, no opinion. Just state the fact(s) clearly.

Real Exam Examples
  • Outline one assumption of the biological approach. [4] Hassett
  • Outline one ethical issue in the study by Piliavin et al. and suggest how it could be overcome. [4] Piliavin
  • Outline one ethical strength of this study. [2] Hassett
Model Answer (4 marks — Outline one assumption of the biological approach)

One assumption is that behaviour can be explained in terms of biological structures and processes, such as hormones, brain anatomy, and genetics. For example, in Hassett et al.'s study, the researchers hypothesised that toy preferences in monkeys could be explained by biological differences in androgen exposure during prenatal development, rather than by social learning. This shows that the biological approach assumes behaviour has a physiological basis that can be observed and measured.

Why 4 marks: 1 mark for naming the assumption + 1 mark for explaining it + 1 mark for linking to the study + 1 mark for the specific example (androgen exposure).

2.3 Describe 2–6 marks AO1

Structured factual recall. More detail than Outline. For 6 marks, you need specific data points.

Real Exam Examples
  • Describe the sample used in Group 1 (AS/HFA). [4] Baron-Cohen
  • Describe the results of Hassett et al.'s study. [6] Hassett
  • Describe two ways in which the procedure used by Piliavin et al. was standardised. [4] Piliavin
  • Describe the Comfortable Interpersonal Distance (CID) paradigm used in Experiment 1. [6] Perry
Model Answer (6 marks — Describe the results of Hassett et al.)

Hassett et al. found that female monkeys showed a significantly greater preference for plush toys (typically feminine) than males, while males showed a greater preference for wheeled toys (typically masculine). Specifically, the mean percentage of time interacting with plush toys was higher for females, and the mean percentage for wheeled toys was higher for males. Both male and female monkeys showed low interest in neutral toys. The effect size for the wheeled-toy preference was large. These results mirrored human gender-typed toy preferences, suggesting a biological rather than purely social explanation.

Why 6 marks: Specific data (percentages/means), both genders covered, comparison to human data, and a brief interpretation linking back to the aim.

2.4 Explain / Suggest 2–6 marks AO2

Application of knowledge — either explaining why something matters or suggesting how to improve/change something. These require light structure: identify the concept, then apply it.

Real Exam Examples
  • Explain why Biyu's teacher has said she cannot deceive participants. [3] Hölzel
  • Suggest why Karl will not be able to conclude cause and effect. [2] Dement
  • Explain how to operationalise the variables of sleeping and eating. [4] Dement
  • How could the methodology of this study be improved? [6] Hassett
  • Suggest two problems with collecting data about hours of sleep. [4] Dement
Model Answer (4 marks — Explain how to operationalise sleeping and eating)

Eating could be operationalised as the number of calories consumed during the day, recorded using a food diary completed at each meal. Sleeping could be operationalised as the number of hours of sleep that night, measured objectively with an actigraph worn on the wrist. Stating the exact measure for each variable makes them testable and measurable, so another researcher could replicate the study using the same definitions.

Why 4 marks: 1 mark per operationalised variable (2) + 1 mark for specifying the measurement tool + 1 mark for explaining why operationalisation matters.

2.5 Evaluate 6–10 marks AO3

This is where the framework matters most. You must give balanced strengths and weaknesses, each with evidence from the study.

Real Exam Examples
  • Evaluate the study by Piliavin et al., including two strengths and two weaknesses. [6] Piliavin
  • Evaluate one strength and one weakness of this study. [10] Perry
  • Evaluate the study by Dement and Kleitman, including two strengths and two weaknesses. [10] Dement
  • Explain one strength of using a laboratory experiment in this study. [3] Baron-Cohen
Notice the wording: "including two strengths and two weaknesses" tells you exactly how many points to make. If the question says this, write exactly 2+2. If it doesn't specify, aim for balanced coverage.

2.6 Discuss / Assess / To what extent 8–12 marks AO3

Extended evaluation. Same as Evaluate, but you also need a position and a conclusion. These are the highest-mark questions — and the ones where students lose the most marks by not structuring their answer.

Real Exam Examples
  • 'Gender differences in behaviour are entirely due to socialisation.' To what extent does Hassett et al.'s study support this statement? [12] Hassett
  • Discuss the nature-nurture debate in relation to this study. [8] Hassett
  • Assess whether the Eyes test has good reliability and validity. [8] Baron-Cohen
  • To what extent do the findings support the view that people with ASD have a specific deficit in theory of mind? [8] Baron-Cohen
  • Discuss the ethical issues involved in this type of research. [6] Baron-Cohen

2.7 Compare 4 marks AO3

A specific question type that requires you to identify similarities and/or differences between two studies or approaches.

Real Exam Example
  • Compare the biological approach and the learning approach to show two ways in which they are different. Use the study by Hassett et al. as an example of the biological approach. [4] Hassett

2.8 Design 10 marks AO2

Design a replication or a new study. This tests your research methods knowledge applied to a Core Study context.

Real Exam Example
  • Design a replication study to test the reliability of Hassett et al.'s findings. [10] Hassett

3. Answer Frameworks (When to Use Them)

3.1 The PEE Chain — For Evaluation (6–12 marks)

Every evaluation point should follow this structure. It's the difference between a 4-mark answer and an 8-mark answer.

P
Point — Name the strength or weakness and identify the syllabus dimension it relates to.
Example: "A strength is the high level of experimental control Control of Variables"
E
Evidence — Give specific detail from THIS study (not a generic point). Use data, procedure details, or design features.
Example: "The study was conducted in a laboratory, so noise and light were controlled. Participants slept in a dark, quiet room and avoided caffeine and alcohol."
E
Explain — Say WHY this matters. Does it improve validity? Reliability? Generalisability? What would happen without this control?
Example: "These controls reduce demand characteristics because participants could not adjust their reports to match the researcher's expectations, which increases the internal validity of the findings."
Full PEE Example (from Dement & Kleitman, 10-mark evaluation)

P: A strength is the high level of experimental control. Control of Variables
E: The study was conducted in a laboratory, so extraneous variables such as noise and light were controlled — participants slept in a dark, quiet room and avoided caffeine and alcohol. They were woken by a loud doorbell so they were roused instantly from any sleep stage. Participant WD was deliberately misled about the stage from which he would be woken.
E: These controls reduced demand characteristics, because participants could not adjust their dream reports to match what they believed the researchers wanted. This increases the internal validity of the findings, as we can be more confident that the dream–REM relationship is genuine rather than an artefact of participant expectations.

3.2 Balanced Structure — For "Evaluate" (6–10 marks)

When the question says "including two strengths and two weaknesses," use this structure:

SectionContentApprox. Marks
Strength 1PEE chain — one strength with syllabus dimension2–3
Strength 2PEE chain — different dimension from Strength 12–3
Weakness 1PEE chain — one weakness with syllabus dimension2–3
Weakness 2PEE chain — different dimension from Weakness 12–3
Conclusion (10+ marks only)One sentence weighing up: which is more significant?1
Choose different dimensions. Don't write two strengths both about "Control of Variables." Pick from different syllabus dimensions (see §5) so your answer shows breadth.

3.3 Position–Evidence–Counter–Conclusion — For "To what extent" (8–12 marks)

These questions ask you to take a position. The framework is slightly different from a standard evaluation:

P
Position — State your initial answer to the question (do the findings support the statement or not?). You don't need to commit fully — "to some extent" or "largely" is fine.
E
Evidence — Give 2–3 points that support your position, using PEE chains with study-specific data.
C
Counter-evidence — Give 1–2 points that go against your position (e.g., limitations of the evidence, alternative explanations). This shows balance.
C
Conclusion — Weigh up: overall, how far does the study support the statement? Refer back to the evidence.
Model Answer Structure (12 marks — Hassett: 'Gender differences are entirely due to socialisation')

P: Hassett et al.'s study does not support the statement that gender differences are entirely due to socialisation. In fact, it provides evidence for a biological basis.

E1 (Supporting biological): The study found that female monkeys preferred plush toys and male monkeys preferred wheeled toys — despite monkeys not being socialised in the way human children are. Validity

E2 (Supporting biological): The researchers linked these preferences to prenatal androgen exposure, suggesting a hormonal rather than social cause. Types of Data

C1 (Counter — socialisation can't be fully ruled out): However, the monkeys lived in social groups and could have learned behaviours through observation of older group members. Validity

C2 (Counter — small sample limits conclusion): The sample was limited to one species (rhesus monkeys), so we cannot be certain the same biological mechanism applies to humans. Sampling

Conclusion: Overall, Hassett et al.'s findings challenge the statement rather than support it. They suggest that at least some gender-typed preferences have a biological component, though socialisation may also play a role in combination.

3.4 Comparison Framework — For "Compare" (4 marks)

Comparison questions are worth exactly 4 marks and require exactly two points of comparison. Each point should show how the two approaches/studies are similar or different.

1
Identify dimension — What aspect are you comparing? (e.g., methods, assumptions, type of data)
2
Show both sides — How does Study A handle this dimension? How does Study B?
3
State the comparison — Is it a similarity or a difference?
Model Answer (4 marks — Compare biological vs learning approach, using Hassett)

Difference 1 — underlying assumption: The biological approach assumes behaviour is caused by physiological processes — Hassett et al. explained toy preferences in terms of prenatal androgen exposure. In contrast, the learning approach assumes behaviour is shaped by experience — Bandura et al. explained aggression as learned through observation and imitation of a model. Validity

Difference 2 — methods of investigation: The biological approach uses physiological measurement — Hassett et al. used a field experiment with cameras and behavioural checklists. The learning approach uses controlled experiments — Bandura et al. observed and counted children's imitation in a laboratory. Types of Data

3.5 Q8 "Two Friends" — The 4-Mark Mini-Evaluation

Every paper has a Q8 that looks like this: "Two friends, X and Y, are discussing [a specific issue] of the study by [researcher]. X says [positive claim], but Y says [negative claim]. Outline why you think either X or Y is correct, using evidence from the study." [4]

This is a mini-evaluation — the same skills as a 10-mark evaluation, but compressed into 4 marks. The question wording "why you think either X or Y is correct" means you choose ONE side and defend it with evidence. You do not need to write both sides.

1
Take a side & name the dimension (1 mark) — State which friend you agree with, and name the concept/dimension/guideline being discussed: validity, ethics (which guideline?), individual vs situational explanation, application to everyday life, generalisability.
Example: "I agree with Eric. The study has ecological validity." Validity
2
Evidence from the study (1 mark) — Give one specific detail from THIS study that supports your side. Not generic — a real feature of the procedure or sample.
Example: "Children are used to watching cartoons and identifying characters, which is exactly what the line-up task required."
3
Explain the link (1 mark) — "This means that..." — say why the evidence makes your side correct.
Example: "This means the task measured a familiar, realistic skill, so children's identification responses are likely to reflect real behaviour rather than confusion with the task itself."
4
Add a second evidence point OR a definition (1 mark) — Either another study detail supporting your side, or a one-line definition of the dimension (worth 1 mark on its own).
Example (second evidence): "In addition, the cartoon foils were rated by three judges to ensure they were similar to the target faces, so the line-up was fair."
Which side should you pick? The side where you can remember the most specific evidence. Both sides are creditworthy in every Q8 — there is no "correct" friend. A student who remembers three details about ethics should argue the ethical side even if they personally think the study was unethical.
Model Answer (4 marks — Fagen: Tenzin says ethical, Ambar says not ethical)

I agree with Tenzin that the study was ethical. The study followed the guideline of numbers of animals used — the sample was kept to a minimum of only 5 elephants. Ethics

The elephants also had constant access to food and water outside of training, and they could walk away if they did not want to participate — so they were not forced or deprived. Ethics

Furthermore, no punishment was used; only positive reinforcement (banana rewards) was used in training. This means the elephants were never subjected to aversive treatment, which supports the view that the study was ethical.

(Scoring: 1 mark for naming a guideline + 1 mark evidence + 1 mark explanation + 1 mark for the second evidence point.)

Model Answer (4 marks — Pozzulo: Eric says valid, Quinn says not valid)

I agree with Eric that the study had validity. It had ecological validity because children are used to watching cartoons and identifying characters, which is close to the real-life skill of identifying people they have seen. Validity

The study also had controls: the cartoon foils were rated by three judges to ensure they looked similar to the target faces. This means the line-up was fair, so it was more likely that social pressures — not facial similarity — affected identification rates. Control of Variables

(Scoring: 1 mark identification + 1 mark evidence + 1 mark "this means" explanation + 1 mark second evidence/explanation.)

Common Q8 mistakes: (1) Writing both sides — you only need one, and half the answer earns no extra marks. (2) Arguing without study evidence — "the study is ethical because elephants are treated well" gets 1 mark; "the study kept animals to a minimum of 5 and used only positive reinforcement" gets 3. (3) Not naming the dimension/guideline — the first mark is for identifying what you are evaluating.

4. When NOT to Use a Framework

Over-structuring simple questions wastes time and can actually lose marks if the examiner thinks you're padding. Here's when to just answer directly:

Questions That Don't Need a Framework
Question TypeMarksWhat to Do Instead
State / Define / Identify1–2Write the key term or fact. One sentence. Done.
Outline one...2Brief description — 2–3 sentences. No PEE needed.
Describe the sample / results4–6Structured recall: list the facts with specific data. No evaluation.
Suggest one way to...2Give one suggestion + one sentence of explanation.
Explain why...2–3Cause-and-effect: "X is a problem because Y, which means Z."
Over-framework example (BAD):
Question: State the type of problem Biyu is concerned about. [1 mark]
Bad answer: "The problem Biyu is concerned about relates to the issue of demand characteristics. Demand characteristics are cues in a study that allow participants to guess the aim. This is important because if participants know the aim, they may alter their behaviour to please the researcher, which would reduce the validity of the findings. Therefore, the problem is demand characteristics."
Good answer: "Demand characteristics."

The bad answer wastes 4 lines on a 1-mark question. The examiner only needs the term.

5. Evaluation Toolkit — Syllabus Dimensions

Every evaluation point should be tagged with one of these six dimensions. Using them ensures you don't repeat yourself and shows the examiner you understand why each point matters.

Control of Variables Control of Variables

What it means: Did the researcher control extraneous variables? Was the procedure standardised?

Strength if: Lab setting, standardised instructions, controlled environment, counterbalancing, control group.

Weakness if: Field experiment (less control), uncontrolled variables, quasi-experiment (can't randomly allocate).

Key studies: Dement (lab, high control), Andrade (standardised procedure), Milgram (standardised prods), Piliavin (field, low control), Baron-Cohen (lab but quasi-experiment).

Types of Data Types of Data

What it means: Was the data quantitative, qualitative, objective, or subjective?

Strength if: Objective physiological measures (EEG, MRI), quantitative data that can be statistically analysed, behavioural checklists.

Weakness if: Relies on self-report, subjective interpretation, no qualitative data to explain why behaviour occurred.

Key studies: Dement (EEG/EOG objective), Hölzel (MRI objective), Andrade (quantitative recall scores), Milgram (quantitative + qualitative remarks), Bandura (observation checklists).

Sampling Sampling

What it means: How were participants selected? Is the sample representative?

Strength if: Large sample, matched groups, multiple comparison groups, random assignment.

Weakness if: Small sample (N<20), self-selected/volunteer sample, one gender only, opportunity sample, culturally homogeneous.

Key studies: Baron-Cohen (15 AS/HFA, all male — weakness), Dement (N=9 — weakness), Hölzel (16, self-selected — weakness), Milgram (40, varied occupations — strength), Bandura (72 children, matched for aggression — strength).

Ethics Ethics

What it means: Were participants treated ethically? Consider: informed consent, deception, protection from harm, right to withdraw, debriefing, confidentiality.

Strength if: Informed consent obtained, debriefing provided, right to withdraw clearly stated, minimal deception.

Weakness if: Deception (especially active lies), no right to withdraw, psychological harm, no debriefing, vulnerable participants.

Key studies: Milgram (severe deception + harm), Piliavin (no consent, deception), Dement (WD deception), Andrade (mild deception), Baron-Cohen (ethical — informed consent, anonymous data).

Validity Validity

What it means: Is the study measuring what it claims to measure? Can findings be generalised?

Types:

  • Internal validity: Are the results due to the IV, or confounding variables?
  • Ecological validity: Does the setting reflect real life?
  • Population validity: Can findings generalise to other groups?
  • Construct validity: Does the measure truly capture the concept?

Key studies: Dement (low ecological validity — lab sleep), Piliavin (high ecological validity — real subway), Baron-Cohen (low ecological — eyes only, not whole face), Perry (low ecological — CID not real social interaction).

Reliability & Replicability Reliability & Replicability

What it means: Would the study produce the same results if repeated? Is the procedure detailed enough to replicate?

Strength if: Standardised procedure, detailed method section, objective measures, pilot study conducted, inter-rater reliability checked.

Weakness if: Procedure not fully described, subjective measures, no reliability check, one-off measurement.

Key studies: Dement (standardised EEG/EOG — strength), Baron-Cohen (pilot study + 8 judges validated target words — strength), Bandura (standardised observation with 5-second intervals — strength), Saavedra (single case study — hard to replicate — weakness).

6. High-Frequency Knowledge Points Bank

These are the concepts that appear across multiple studies. If you can explain these well, you can answer evaluation questions on any of the 12 Core Studies.

6.1 Concepts That Work Across Almost Every Study

Lab vs Field Experiment

Trade-off: Lab = high control, low ecological validity. Field = high ecological validity, low control.

Use for: Dement, Andrade, Baron-Cohen, Milgram, Bandura (lab) vs Piliavin, Hassett (field)

Sampling Issues

Volunteer bias: People who volunteer may differ from the general population.

Small sample: N<20 limits generalisability.

Use for: Baron-Cohen (N=15), Dement (N=9), Hölzel (N=16)

Demand Characteristics

Cues that let participants guess the aim. They may then behave differently — reducing validity.

Use for: Milgram, Andrade, Baron-Cohen, Dement

Ethical Issues

Deception, informed consent, protection from harm, right to withdraw, debriefing.

Use for: Milgram (severe), Piliavin (no consent), Dement (WD), Andrade (mild)

Ecological Validity

Can the findings generalise to real life? Artificial tasks = lower EV.

Use for: Dement (lab sleep), Baron-Cohen (eyes only), Perry (CID task)

Quantitative vs Qualitative

Quantitative = numbers (scores, percentages). Qualitative = words (interviews, remarks). Both have strengths.

Use for: Milgram (both), Bandura (quant), Saavedra (qual), Andrade (quant)

Correlation ≠ Causation

A correlation shows a relationship, but doesn't prove one variable causes the other. Third variables may be involved.

Use for: Dement (r values), Baron-Cohen (AQ–Eyes Test correlation), Hölzel (grey matter–homework)

Standardised Procedure

All participants experience the same conditions, instructions, and measures. This improves reliability.

Use for: Milgram (same prods), Dement (same EEG setup), Bandura (same room), Andrade (same tape)

6.3 Procedure Detail Questions — The Step-by-Step Bank

Procedure questions are the most unpredictable in Section A — over half of Section A questions involve procedure in some way. But the answers are pure recall: memorise the steps in order, name the objects/numbers, and you get the marks. Click each question to reveal the credited steps (based on real mark schemes).

Bandura et al. — Describe the procedure for the 'Aggression Arousal' phase. 4–5 Bandura
  • The child was brought to an anteroom containing attractive toys (e.g. fire engine, locomotive, fighter jet, spinning top, doll, doll carriage).
  • They were told they could play with them.
  • As soon as they began to play (usually after about 2 minutes), the experimenter stopped them and took the toys away.
  • The experimenter explained that she did not let just anyone play with these toys — they were reserved for other children.
  • The children were told the toys were her "very best" toys.
  • The experimenter sat away from the child during this phase and completed paperwork.
Andrade — Describe the procedure for a participant in the doodling condition. 5 Andrade
  • Participants were recruited after completing a different, unrelated study, and taken into a visually dull room.
  • They listened to a tape recording of a monotonous telephone message (a party invitation).
  • While listening, the doodling group was asked to shade in the squares and circles on a piece of paper.
  • They were told it did not matter how neat or how quickly — it was to relieve boredom.
  • They were told they did not need to remember the message, but were instructed to write down the names of the party-goers.
  • After the call, participants engaged in a one-minute conversation with the experimenter, then wrote down the party-goers' names and the places.
  • The test on places was a surprise — they had not been told about it. (Then debriefed and asked if they suspected a memory test.)
Hassett et al. — Describe the procedure for one trial (plush vs wheeled toy). 4 Hassett
  • Monkeys were initially kept indoors; one plush toy and one wheeled toy were already placed in the outdoor area, separated by 10 metres.
  • Left/right placement of the toys was counterbalanced per trial.
  • Monkeys were released into the outdoor area; any interaction was videotaped (two separate cameras, one per toy).
  • Each trial lasted 25 minutes.
  • Examples of plush toys: Winnie-the-Pooh, Raggedy-Ann, koala, armadillo, bear, Scooby-Doo. Wheeled toys: wagon, truck, car, construction vehicle, shopping cart.
Milgram — Describe the procedure from the hat draw until the learning task began. 6 Milgram
  • Both pieces of paper drawn from the hat said "teacher" — the draw was rigged; the true participant always became the teacher.
  • Both men went into an adjacent room, where the learner was strapped to an "electric chair" (to prevent excessive movement).
  • An electrode was attached to the learner's wrist, with electrode paste "to prevent blisters/burns".
  • They were told the electrode was connected to the shock generator and that the shocks would be painful but cause no permanent damage.
  • The teacher (real participant) received a sample shock of 45 V by pressing the third button on the generator.
Milgram — Describe the shock generator (other than the 30 lever switches). 4 Milgram
  • Each switch was clearly labelled with a voltage; volts ranged from 15 V to 450 V in 15 V steps.
  • Groups of four voltage levels had verbal designations (Slight Shock → Danger: Severe Shock); the last two were labelled simply "XXX".
  • Pressing a switch produced a red light, an electric buzzing sound, and lit a blue light labelled "voltage energizer".
  • The generator was connected to the learner by electrodes/wires that were "supposed" to send a shock.
Piliavin et al. — Describe the procedure from when the team boarded until the victim collapsed. 6 Piliavin
  • Each trial team was always 2 males and 2 females, who boarded the train using different doors.
  • The experimental car (and its location on the train) was varied between trials.
  • The females sat outside the critical area and recorded data as covertly as possible during the ride.
  • The male victim/model always stood, next to the pole in the centre of the critical area.
  • The model (in the drunk/ill/cane condition) went to the adjacent or critical area depending on the condition.
  • The victim waited 70 seconds after the train left the station before collapsing.
Pozzulo et al. — Describe the 'line-up presentation' in the target-absent condition. 5 Pozzulo
  • The target was replaced with a similar foil (foil had similar features to the target).
  • All pictures were shown simultaneously; there were four photographs/foils in the line-up.
  • The target and target-replacement were always in the same position; the target's position in the line-up was randomised.
  • A silhouette was included in the line-up to represent the possibility of an absent target.
  • Participants watched four videos (presented on a laptop/screen in random order), with a photo array after each video; none of the photographs had appeared in the videos.
Fagen et al. — Describe how the elephants were trained to perform the 'trunk up' task. 4–5 Fagen
  • Lure and shaping techniques were used: the elephant was tempted to perform the behaviour by placing a reward (banana pieces) a few inches from its forehead.
  • The elephant could only reach the banana by lifting its trunk up.
  • If the elephant was adult/too big, the lure was held at the end of the trainer's arm.
  • The position of the trunk was shaped, using increasing height until "trunk up" was achieved.
How to revise these: For each study, learn the procedure as a numbered sequence (1→2→3...) rather than a paragraph. Then test yourself by covering the answers and recalling the steps — plus any numbers (70 seconds, 25 minutes, 45 V, 10 metres). Numbers are where the AO1 marks live.

7.3 AO3 — Evaluation (Evaluate, Discuss, Assess)

MarksWhat Gets You There
1–2One or two brief evaluation points, may be generic
3–4Two–three points with some study-specific evidence, but may lack explanation of why
5–6Balanced evaluation (strengths + weaknesses) with specific study evidence and explanation of impact
7–8As above, plus use of syllabus dimensions, good balance, and a brief conclusion
9–10Comprehensive evaluation — 4+ developed PEE points, different dimensions, clear conclusion weighing up the evidence
11–12All of the above, plus nuanced position-taking, counter-arguments, and sophisticated reasoning
The #1 reason students lose marks on evaluation: Generic points. "The sample was small" gets 1 mark. "The sample was small — only 9 participants, all volunteers for a dream study, who may already remember dreams better than average — which limits generalisability to the wider population" gets 3 marks. Always be specific to the study.

8. Common Pitfalls

1. Writing too much for low-mark questions. A 1-mark "State" question needs one word. A 2-mark "Outline" needs 2 sentences. Don't write a paragraph — you're wasting time you need for the 10-mark questions at the end.
2. Generic evaluation points. "The study lacked ecological validity" is not enough. You must say why — what was artificial? What specific feature made it unrealistic? Use details from the study.
3. Comparing to other studies in evaluation. Unless the question explicitly asks you to compare, evaluation should focus on the study itself. Don't write "unlike Bandura, Milgram..." in a Milgram evaluation question. This is a CIE 9990 requirement — use only elements from the study being evaluated.
4. No conclusion for 10+ mark questions. If you're asked to evaluate in 10+ marks, you must end with a conclusion sentence that weighs up the strengths and weaknesses. Without it, you cap at 8 marks.
5. All strengths or all weaknesses. "Evaluate" requires balance. Even if you think the study is excellent, you must acknowledge weaknesses. A one-sided answer caps at 4–5 marks for a 10-mark question.
6. Repeating the same dimension. If both your strengths are about "Control of Variables," you only get credit for one. Choose different syllabus dimensions for each point.
7. Not reading the question carefully. "Including two strengths and two weaknesses" means exactly that — not three strengths and one weakness. "Outline one..." means one, not two. The question tells you the structure.
8. Forgetting to use data. In Describe questions, examiners want to see specific numbers, percentages, means, and p-values. Without data, your description is vague and earns fewer marks.

9. Quick Reference Cards

1–2 marks: State / Define

No framework. Write the fact.
1 mark = 1 key term
2 marks = term + brief explanation

Example: "Demand characteristics." [1]
Example: "A field experiment is conducted in a natural setting where the IV is manipulated." [2]

2–4 marks: Outline / Describe

No evaluation. Structured recall.
2 marks: brief description
4 marks: detailed, with specific facts
6 marks: comprehensive, with data

Include: numbers, sample details, procedure specifics

2–6 marks: Explain / Suggest

Light structure. Identify concept → apply to scenario → explain link.
No need for PEE chain.

Example: "Eating could be operationalised as calories consumed, recorded via food diary." [4]

6–10 marks: Evaluate

Full PEE framework.
2 strengths + 2 weaknesses
Each: Point → Evidence → Explain
10+ marks: add conclusion

Use different syllabus dimensions for each point

8–12 marks: Discuss / Assess

Position framework.
Position → Evidence (2-3) → Counter (1-2) → Conclusion
Must take a stance and weigh both sides

"To what extent..." = take a position

4 marks: Compare

Two comparison points.
Each: Identify dimension → Show Study A → Show Study B → State similarity/difference

Always use both studies/approaches

10.4 What the Map Tells You — The 8 Golden Patterns

1. Q10 = Evaluation, always

Every series ends with a 10-mark "two strengths and two weaknesses" evaluation. Bandura appeared most often (3 times across 2024–2025) — but any study can come up. Prepare 4 PEE points per study, each on a different dimension.

2. Q9 = Similarities & Differences, always

Always 8 marks: describe one feature (sample, procedure, EEG, FFMQ, victim...) then compare with another study. Dement appeared most (EEG, dream, dream reports). Note: "Do not refer to the sample" appears regularly — read the restriction!

3. Q5 = Approach Assumptions, almost always

2024: bio=Hölzel, social=Perry, learning=Bandura, cognitive=Pozzulo. 2025: bio=Hölzel, social=Piliavin, learning=Saavedra, cognitive=Baron-Cohen. Learn all 4 assumptions × 2 points — this is free marks.

4. Q8 = "Two friends", always

Always the 4-mark mini-evaluation. 2024 favoured generalisability / ethics / validity; 2025 heavily favoured application to everyday life. Framework in §3.5.

5. Procedure = the biggest question pool

Procedure details appear at Q1, Q2, Q4, Q6, Q7 — over half of Section A. It cannot be predicted, so memorise the step sequences for all 12 studies (§6.3). Dement and Saavedra were under-tested for procedure before 2024 — worth extra attention.

6. Q7 shifts by year

2024: application to everyday life (via procedure detail) + "problem of using children". 2025: individual vs situational explanation, nature vs nurture, procedure detail. Practise all three shapes.

7. Results recur every year

Hassett, Dement, Perry and Pozzulo results appeared in both 2024 and 2025. Know at least one precise number/percentage per study — results questions are pure recall of data.

8. Sample details never stop

Hassett, Fagen, Hölzel and Pozzulo sample details repeat constantly. The easiest preparation: remember 2 participant characteristics + the sampling method for every study.
