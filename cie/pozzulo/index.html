1️⃣ Background（研究背景）

The psychology under investigation. Human memory works like a computer in some respects, but differs critically in how stored data can be accidentally altered or damaged. Unlike a computer, which reproduces an error-free copy of stored information, human memory is reconstructive — we may feel our recall is perfectly accurate, yet cognitive psychologists have demonstrated that memories can be distorted by information encountered during and after encoding.

Additional information given after an event, called post-event information（事件后信息）, can produce "false memories（错误记忆）". A witness may confidently believe these false memories to be true. Loftus and Palmer (1974) showed this in a classic experiment: participants who watched a video of a car accident estimated faster speeds when asked how fast the cars were going when they "smashed into" each other versus when they "hit" each other.

📌 KEY TERMS

  • False memory（错误记忆）: stored information an individual believes to be accurate but which is actually the consequence of later additional, untrue information (e.g., leading questions posed to an eyewitness).
  • Eyewitness testimony（目击者证词）: evidence provided by someone who has seen (or heard) a crime being committed, used by the legal system to identify perpetrators.
  • Line-up（排队指认 / 列队指认）: a police procedure where a witness is shown a row of people or array of photographs and asked to identify the perpetrator (who may or may not be present).
  • False positive response（错误肯定反应）: giving an affirmative but incorrect answer — e.g., mistakenly identifying an innocent person when the real culprit is absent from the line-up.

Children as well as adults can serve as eyewitnesses. Sometimes a child may be the only witness to a crime, so the legal system must rely on their eyewitness testimony（目击者证词）. For this evidence to be useful, children need to be reliable witnesses — but are they?

1.1 Child Witnesses and Line-ups

Pozzulo et al. recognised that cognitive effects, such as those caused by post-event information (including the way questions are phrased), can produce errors in children's decision-making. Their earlier research (Pozzulo & Lindsay, 1997) showed that children were less likely than adults to say "I don't know" in response to a question, even when they knew this was a permissible option.

In a line-up（排队指认）, a witness is asked to identify the perpetrator from a selection of individuals (whether or not the culprit is actually present). However, this system can produce errors leading to miscarriages of justice. Pozzulo and Lindsay (1998) found that when the culprit is not among the people shown, children are more likely than adults to identify an innocent person — a false positive response（错误肯定反应）.

1.2 Why Do Children Make More Errors?

This study focused on social effects on child witnesses. Pozzulo et al. proposed several social factors that may lead children to make incorrect decisions:

  • If a child is asked to select the culprit from a line-up, they may feel they must choose someone. Not making a choice (saying the culprit isn't there) might feel like a "non-response" or failure to comply.
  • A child is likely to view the adult asking questions as an authority figure（权威人物）, especially a police officer, making them more likely to comply.
  • Children may feel greater pressure（压力） to make a choice than adults — they want to please the adult and may fear getting into trouble for not selecting someone.

1.3 Four Predictions

The study aimed to test four predictions:

  1. Children will be as good as adults at identifying cartoon faces in a target-present line-up（目标存在队列）.
  2. Children will be worse than adults at identifying human faces in a target-present line-up.
  3. Children will be worse than adults at rejecting cartoon faces in a target-absent line-up（目标缺失队列）.
  4. Children will be worse than adults at rejecting human faces in a target-absent line-up.
💡 Core logic（核心逻辑）: If children identify cartoon faces at nearly 100% accuracy (because cartoons are familiar and cognitively easy), yet still show significantly lower correct-rejection rates than adults in target-absent conditions → this suggests errors are driven by social factors rather than cognitive factors. This is the central hypothesis of the study!

2️⃣ Aim（研究目的）

Social factors may exert a stronger influence on child witnesses than on adults. One eyewitness test（目击者测试） is identification of a suspect from a 'target-present' line-up（目标存在队列）, where the culprit is among the faces presented. Another test is the ability to correctly reject faces when the culprit is absent — a 'target-absent' line-up（目标缺失队列）.

To isolate social versus cognitive factors, Pozzulo et al. minimised cognitive demands by comparing performance on cartoon character（卡通人物） line-ups. Cartoon characters are familiar, so both children and adults should identify them with approximately 100% accuracy — a cognitively easy task requiring only matching an existing memory to visible faces. However, in a target-absent task, matching is impossible, forcing a selection. Faced with this harder task, children should rely more heavily on social factors and make more errors than adults.

Aim in one sentence 一句话 aim：
To test whether the higher false-positive error rate observed in child witnesses (compared with adults) is due to cognitive factors (e.g. weaker memory) or social factors (e.g. pressure to choose from a line-up), by comparing children's and adults' performance in target-present vs. target-absent line-ups with both familiar cartoon and unfamiliar human faces.
（通过比较儿童和成人在目标存在与目标缺失队列（卡通与真人面孔）中的表现，探究儿童错误肯定率更高是源于认知因素还是社会因素。）
§2 研究目的：分离认知与社会因素

核心思路：如果能找到一个任务让儿童和成人的记忆能力相当（都接近 100%），那么剩下的差异只能归因于社会因素。

巧妙设计：选择熟悉的卡通人物（Dora、Diego）作为目标 → 识别认知需求极低，几乎是完美匹配 → 儿童识别率 ≈ 100% → 此时任何剩余错误都不是认知问题。

反之：在目标缺失时，儿童正确拒绝率显著低于成人 → 这是社会压力造成的（儿童感到"必须选一个"）。

▶ Exam Key Points — Aim AO1
  • Aim = investigate whether child false positives are caused by cognitive vs. social factors Must Know
  • Clever manipulation: cartoons = familiar (low cognitive demand) vs. humans = unfamiliar (high cognitive demand) Must Know
  • Target-present = identifying culprit (cognition-focused); target-absent = correctly rejecting (social-pressure-focused) Must Know
  • AO2: Aim is testable — predictions are clear and falsifiable, showing good internal validity AO2 Gold

3.1 Research Design

This was a laboratory experiment（实验室实验）. Participants completed the task in a laboratory setting, whereas real line-ups take place in official venues such as police stations.

There were three independent variables（自变量 / IVs）:

5️⃣ Procedure（研究程序）

5.1 Stimulus Materials

Human face targets: Two Caucasian students were filmed for two types of stimulus material:

  • Videos: 6-second colour clips of everyday tasks (female brushing her hair; male putting on his coat), showing 2–3 seconds of the individual's face, no sound.
  • Photoarrays (line-ups): Foils were selected from a pool of 90 female and 90 male faces. For each target, four "foil" photographs were selected by three raters to match the target in general facial structure, hair length and colour. All photos were cropped to show only face, neck and shoulders, in black and white.

Cartoon face targets:

  • Dora the Explorer talking to the audience
  • Go, Diego, Go! putting on gloves for safety

Cartoon videos were also 6-second colour clips without sound. Foil images were selected from a vast number of cartoon images on the internet: three raters judged the similarity of approximately ten cartoons for each target, and the four receiving the highest similarity ratings (most similar to least similar) were selected — again in terms of general facial structure, hair length and colour. All photos were in black and white to eliminate colour-based recognition cues.

🔗 Research Methods Link: Inter-rater Reliability

Pozzulo et al. used three raters to select foils for both human and cartoon line-ups. This is an example of an inter-rater procedure: multiple independent judges rating the same stimuli reduce the subjectivity of one person choosing foils alone, improving the reliability of stimulus selection — if foils were too dissimilar, the target would be obvious and the task trivially easy (invalid).

⚠️ Exam caution: the paper describes "three raters" judging similarity but does not report an inter-rater reliability coefficient (e.g., percentage agreement). So in the exam, write:

"Foils were selected by three raters who judged similarity in terms of facial structure, hair length and colour, which reduced subjectivity in the selection of line-up materials."

Do not write "inter-rater reliability was calculated/measured" — that would be inaccurate for this study.

5.2 Experimental Procedure

📋 Research Methods Context

Line-ups are used worldwide in legal cases. Procedures are highly controlled to minimise errors — e.g., ensuring photographs are uniform in size and all in colour or all in black and white. One unresolved issue: people are less accurate at identifying individuals of a different ethnic group from themselves (cross-race effect). Researchers must therefore consider participant ethnicity and line-up member ethnicity as potential confounding variables.

For Children:

  1. Three female experimenters wore professional-casual clothing (not overly formal) to reduce authority-figure pressure.
  2. Each child was tested individually after making crafts together to build rapport（融洽关系） and comfort.
  3. The experimenter explained that the child could change their mind at any time and would not get into trouble.
  4. The child watched one video clip (order randomised across participants).
  5. After the clip, a free recall filler question: "What did the cartoon character/person look like?" followed twice by "Do you remember anything else?"
  6. The experimenter displayed the corresponding line-up on a laptop (PowerPoint).
  7. Instruction: "The person you saw may or may not be there. If the correct person is NOT there, please point to the silhouetted box."
  8. The procedure repeated for all 4 videos (Dora + Diego + Female Human + Male Human), with reminders that the target may be absent.
  9. After completion, children received crayons and a colouring book as a thank-you gift.

For Adults:

  1. Gave written consent for a study about "memory".
  2. Watched each video clip individually.
  3. After each clip, wrote responses to free recall questions ("What did the cartoon character/person look like?" + "Do you remember anything else?").
  4. Viewed the line-up on laptop and wrote down their identification response.
  5. Same 4-video sequence as children, order counterbalanced.

Key control features:

  • Line-up position of target was randomised per participant
  • Video presentation order was counterbalanced（平衡/抵消）
  • All photos in black & white to eliminate colour cues
  • Target-present line-ups: target + 3 foils
  • Target-absent line-ups: 4 foils (similar-looking replacement in original target position)
  • Each line-up included a blank silhouette box representing "not here" option

7️⃣ Conclusion（结论）

As predicted, children easily identified the correct face in target-present line-ups with cartoons, achieving nearly 100% accuracy — a ceiling effect（天花板效应）. Pozzulo et al. concluded that any errors in target-absent line-ups for cartoons must result from social factors rather than cognitive factors.

This cannot be explained by faulty memory for cartoon faces — children's memory was excellent. The most likely social factor driving children's lower correct rejection rate is the expectation that they should make a selection rather than a "non-selection" (correctly stating that the target is absent).

Furthermore, the similar pattern observed for human faces suggests that social demands also explain the difference between children and adults in rejection rates for human targets. Importantly, these effects of cognitive versus social demands are relative, not absolute: both identification and rejection tasks are influenced by both types of factors to some degree. The results simply suggest that for children, social factors play a larger role in decision-making during target-absent line-ups than during target-present ones.

8️⃣ Strengths & Weaknesses（优缺点）

以下五段 Strengths 与四段 Weaknesses 是按 AO2 L4 等级（mechanism chained + research-specific evidence）写就的范文段落。学生可直接背段落、考场上按同一结构套用。（按 4 档评分，L4 = 机制链点名 + 具体研究证据；L1–L3 见顶部 tip）

✅ Strengths 优点 （L4 范文段落）

① Mixed factorial design → controls individual differences → high internal validity. A 2 (age: children vs. adults) × 2 (target: cartoon vs. human) × 2 (line-up type: target-present vs. target-absent) mixed factorial design was used. Age was a between-subjects factor (independent measures: N=59 children and N=53 adults), while target type and line-up type were within-subjects factors (repeated measures). This combination controls for individual differences such as baseline memory, motivation, and prior experience with line-ups, which would otherwise be confounding variables. As a result, any differences in identification and rejection rates can be more confidently attributed to age and target type rather than to participant variability, increasing the internal validity of the study. 混合因子设计：年龄 between + 目标/lineup 类型 within → 控制个体差异 → 提升内部效度。

② Inclusion of cartoon and human stimuli → separates cognitive from social factors → unique methodological strength. The researchers cleverly compared familiar cartoon characters (Dora and Diego) against unfamiliar human faces (a 22-year-old female brushing her hair; a 22-year-old male putting on his coat) within the same participants. This contrast separated cognitive demand (memory for familiar vs. unfamiliar faces) from social pressure (the implicit demand to "pick someone" from the array). Children's near-perfect 99% identification of cartoons showed their memory was intact; their significantly lower target-absent rejection rate (74%) showed that social pressure — not cognitive immaturity — was the cause of false positives. This double-contrast design is the study's greatest methodological strength because it allows a clean theoretical attribution that single-stimulus studies cannot achieve. 卡通 vs 人脸对比 → 分离"认知记忆"与"社会压力" → 直接验证假设的核心设计。

③ Standardised procedure → replicable → high reliability. All participants were tested individually in the same controlled environment, viewing the same four 6-second video clips and the same line-up format (simultaneous presentation via PowerPoint on a 13-inch laptop). Identical instructions were given to every participant: "The person/cartoon from the video may or may not be here. If you see the person/cartoon please point to the photo. If you do not see the person/cartoon, please point to this box." This high degree of standardisation makes the procedure easy to replicate, increasing reliability across participants and across replications. 标准化流程（统一指示、设备、视频、lineup 格式） → 可复制 → reliability。

④ Quantitative data + chi-square (χ²) testing → objective statistical inference. The dependent variables were operationalised as correct identification rate (for target-present line-ups) and correct rejection rate (for target-absent line-ups), both on a 0–1 scale. This yielded objective quantitative data suitable for chi-square (χ²) statistical testing, allowing the researchers to verify all four predictions with reported significance levels (e.g., χ²=18.83, p=.001 for human-face identification; χ²=7.66, p=.01 for cartoon-face rejection). Quantitative data permit precise group comparisons and can be generalised from sample to population. 定量 DV（0–1 比例）+ χ² 检验 → 客观、可推论、p 值可读。

⑤ Inter-rater reliability for foil selection → objective foil matching. Foil photographs were selected by three raters who judged approximately 10 candidate photographs per target on three similarity dimensions: facial structure, hair length, and hair colour. The four cartoons (and four human faces) receiving the highest similarity scores were used. Using multiple independent raters introduces inter-rater reliability, reducing subjective bias in foil selection and ensuring the foils were genuinely similar to the target — preventing the obvious target from "standing out" by chance, which would otherwise artificially inflate identification rates. 三名评分员选 foils（面部结构/发长/发色）→ inter-rater reliability → foils 客观可比。

⚠️ Weaknesses 缺点 （L4 范文段落）

① Low ecological validity → generalisability to real forensic settings is limited. The study was conducted in a quiet laboratory using non-crime-related stimuli (popular cartoons and ordinary actions such as hair-brushing and putting on a coat). Real eyewitness situations, however, involve intense emotional stress, fear of a hostile perpetrator, and rapid event sequences — all of which significantly affect memory encoding and decision-making. Because the laboratory lacked this realism, the findings may not generalise to high-stress forensic contexts where children's error patterns could be qualitatively different (e.g., memory collapse, freezing, greater reliance on authority figures). This reduces the study's ecological validity and therefore its external validity. 实验室环境 vs 真实犯罪现场 → 缺乏情绪压力 → ecological validity 低。

② Demand characteristics → participants may have guessed the hypothesis → internal validity reduced. Adults were told the study was about "memory", while children were told it was about "TV shows and computer games". Adult participants may have guessed the underlying hypothesis (that social pressure, not memory, drives false positives in children) and over-performed on the rejection trials; children may have picked someone simply to please the female experimenter, who was dressed in smart-casual clothing to reduce authority cues. This could artificially narrow or widen the child–adult gap, threatening the internal validity of the age comparison. 成人 vs 儿童收到不同的 cover story → 参与者可能猜测假设 → 内部效度受损。

③ Ethnic homogeneity of targets and foils → cross-race effect → low generalisability. All human targets and foils were Caucasian faces — the two targets were 22-year-old Caucasian university students, and the foils were drawn from pools of 90 female and 90 male faces without ethnic stratification. Real-world line-ups in multicultural societies involve far greater ethnic diversity. Combined with the well-documented cross-race effect (the finding that people are significantly less accurate at identifying faces of a different race), this homogeneity limits generalisability to non-Caucasian populations and to the multicultural legal systems in which child witnesses most often testify, reducing external validity. 全是高加索面孔 + cross-race effect → 不能推广到多元文化人群。

④ Small sample + narrow age range + opportunity sampling → age trend untestable, generalisability limited. The sample (N=59 children aged 4–7; N=53 adults aged 17–30) was modest for statistical analysis, and critically no adolescents aged 8–16 were included, leaving a 10-year gap between the two age groups. This prevents detection of age-related trends: we cannot tell whether false-positive rates decline gradually across development, drop sharply at a specific milestone (e.g., puberty), or stabilise in adolescence. Combined with opportunity sampling (three private schools in Eastern Ontario for children; an Introductory Psychology participant pool for adults), the participants are non-representative of the wider population. The study also uses all-female experimenters, which may interact with participant gender in ways that cannot be ruled out — reducing generalisability. N=59/53 + 8–16 岁空缺 + opportunity sampling + 全女实验员 → 不能确定年龄趋势 + 不可推广。

🔟 Issues & Debates（争论与取向）

10.1 Application to Everyday Life

This study has practical applications. Findings suggest strategies should minimise social demands influencing children's line-up responses, improving child witness reliability and court proceedings — particularly for crimes witnessed only by children or against child victims.

However, the evidence identifies the problem without offering solutions. Further research is needed to improve line-up procedures.

Practical solution — The elimination line-up: One procedure designed to reduce social demands is the elimination line-up（排除法队列） (Pozzulo & Lindsay, 1999). Children make two judgments:

  1. Select the person most similar to the target.
  2. Determine whether this person IS actually the target.

Empirical data show positive results across different ages and conditions (Dempsey & Pozzulo, 2008; Pozzulo et al., 2008–2010). Reliable identification evidence from child eyewitnesses is possible — as reliable as adult evidence — when appropriate procedures are used.

10.2 Individual and Situational Explanations

The core finding — children's elevated false positive（错误肯定） rate in target-absent line-ups — can be understood through two levels of explanation:

🧑 Individual Explanation（个体解释）

From an individual-differences perspective, children's errors may stem from immature cognitive development:

  • Children's brains (especially prefrontal cortex) are still developing; inhibitory control（抑制控制） is weaker, making it harder to resist the impulse to "pick someone".
  • Children's recognition of unfamiliar human faces is significantly poorer than adults' (23% vs. 66%), indicating immature memory encoding and retrieval abilities.
  • Age-related cognitive differences reflect innate developmental processes experienced by all children.

Supporting evidence: Children's human-face identification rate (23%) far below adults' (66%) → genuine cognitive/individual differences exist.

🌍 Situational Explanation（情境解释）

From a situational perspective, children's errors arise primarily from social pressure and situational demands:

  • A line-up is inherently a social situation: an authority figure presents photos, implicitly communicating "please choose one".
  • Children are more susceptible to obedience pressure（服从压力） than adults and don't want to disappoint adults.
  • "I was given a line-up, so I must pick someone" reflects a learned social expectation（社会期望）.

Critical evidence: Children's cartoon-ID = 99%, yet cartoon-rejection = only 74% (vs. adults 94%) → Memory is NOT the problem; the problem is social context!

💡 Study's stance（研究立场）: Pozzulo et al. clearly favour the situational explanation. If errors were driven solely by individual cognitive factors, children's cartoon-condition performance would also be poor (it approached 100%). The large gap between cartoon and human conditions demonstrates that social demands are the primary cause of children's high false positive rate.

10.3 Nature versus Nurture

The nature-nurture debate（先天 vs 后天辩论） asks whether behaviour is determined by genetic/biological factors (nature) or environmental/experiential factors (nurture). Pozzulo et al.'s study offers an interesting case.

🧬 Nature (Biological) Arguments

  • Brain development age differences: 4–7-year-olds' prefrontal cortex is immature; neural networks for impulse control and decision inhibition are still developing, creating physiological difficulty in "rejecting when uncertain".
  • Neural basis of face recognition: The fusiform face area (FFA) is still refining during childhood, explaining why children's unfamiliar face recognition (23%) lags behind adults' (66%).
  • Age as a continuous variable: False positive rates may decline naturally with age, reflecting neural maturation timelines rather than learning processes.

🌱 Nurture (Environmental) Arguments

Stronger evidence supports nurture, consistent with the study's conclusions:

  • Socialised obedience behaviours: Children are taught "listen to adults," "answer questions," "don't disappoint" — these are learned social rules（习得的社交规则）, not innate instincts.
  • The crucial cartoon-condition evidence: Children's cartoon recognition ≈100% (ruling out cognitive deficits), yet rejection rate significantly lower than adults → the issue isn't "can they remember?" but "dare they not choose?" — reflecting socialisation-shaped responses to authority.
  • Cross-cultural variation potential: Different cultures socialise children differently toward authority (e.g., collectivist cultures emphasise compliance more). If replicated across cultures, results may vary with socialisation patterns — further supporting nurture.
💡 Overall judgement（综合判断）: Pozzulo et al.'s study provides stronger support for Nurture（后天）. The logic chain:
① Children's normal memory (cartoon-ID 99%) → rules out cognitive/innate deficits.
② But lower rejection rate (74% vs. adults 94%) → errors originate in decision-making, not memory.
③ Decision-making errors correlate with social pressure (authority figures, selection expectation) → these are products of socialisation.
→ Conclusion: Children's false positive responding is largely a learned social-compliance behaviour, not an innate cognitive limitation.

As the authors acknowledge in their limitations, reality likely involves an interaction between nature and nurture: children's neurodevelopmental level (nature) determines their susceptibility to situational pressure, while specific socialisation experiences (nurture) shape how this susceptibility manifests in line-up contexts.

10.4 Children as Participants

Unlike many psychology studies, Pozzulo et al. used 4–7 year-old children as participants, making this issue particularly salient.

Why use children? — Research necessity

In real forensic contexts, children are often the sole eyewitnesses (e.g., domestic violence, abuse, abduction cases). Adult-only research cannot generalise to child witnesses. Pozzulo et al. chose children because:

  • Previous meta-analyses (Pozzulo & Lindsay, 1998) had identified child-adult differences in line-up performance.
  • They needed to directly test whether these differences are socially (rather than merely cognitively) driven.
  • Only real child data can provide reliable guidance for forensic practice.

1️⃣1️⃣ Summary（总结）

Pozzulo et al.'s study explored factors affecting children's accuracy as eyewitnesses when presented with a line-up. Adult and child participants were tested using a photoarray line-up that tested cognition (remembering and identifying the culprit when present) compared with a task influenced by social factors: a target-absent line-up where the task was rejection rather than identification.

In this situation a child can give a false positive response by incorrectly identifying an innocent person as the perpetrator. The researchers used both cartoon characters and humans as targets and foils. Results showed that children were as good as adults at identifying cartoon characters, but less able to correctly identify humans and less able to correctly reject either type of target.

The tendency of children to choose an incorrect face (cartoon or human) in target-absent line-ups suggests these errors are due to social effects. They cannot be cognitive errors because children's memory for the faces was excellent (cartoon identification ≈100%). Therefore, the social demand of choosing a face when the target was absent must have caused the rejection errors.

Model answer: The chi-square value was χ² = 0.39, with a corresponding p = 0.53 (i.e., a 53% probability of obtaining this difference by chance alone). Because p = 0.53 is much greater than the conventional significance threshold of 0.05, the result is not statistically significant: the difference between children's correct identification rate for cartoon faces (0.99) and adults' (0.95) is small enough to be attributable to chance. This finding supports the researchers' first prediction — that children and adults have comparable correct identification rates for familiar cartoon faces (i.e., cognitive memory for familiar stimuli is intact in young children), which in turn supports the social-factor interpretation of false positives: because the children could identify the target, their later failures to reject a target-absent line-up cannot be blamed on cognitive deficits.

1 Outline the aim of Pozzulo et al.'s study on eyewitness testimony. [2 marks] ▶ Answer
Mark scheme (2 marks): 1 mark for each identifiable point, developed for 2 marks each.

Model answer: Pozzulo et al. aimed to investigate whether children's higher false-positive rate in line-ups (compared with adults) is caused by cognitive factors (e.g., weaker memory for unfamiliar faces) or social factors (e.g., pressure to choose from the array). To test this, the researchers compared child and adult identification accuracy in target-present and target-absent line-ups using both familiar cartoon faces and unfamiliar human faces.
2 Describe the procedure of Pozzulo et al.'s study. You may include the stimuli and what participants had to do. [6 marks] ▶ Answer
Mark scheme (6 marks): 1 mark per developed point. Aim for 3–4 developed points covering stimuli, procedure, and key controls.

Model answer:
  • Stimuli: Two Caucasian students filmed 6-second video clips (female brushing hair; male putting on coat). Two cartoon videos (Dora the Explorer; Go, Diego, Go!). Foil photographs were selected by three raters (pool of 90+90 human faces; ~10 cartoons judged per target, top 4 chosen) on facial structure/hair length/colour, shown in black and white.
  • Children's procedure: Tested individually after a craft activity (rapport-building). Watched one video clip, then answered free-recall questions ("What did the cartoon/person look like?"), then viewed a 5-photo line-up (or 4 foils + blank silhouette) on a laptop and pointed to their selection. Repeated for all 4 videos, with target position randomised.
  • Adults' procedure: Gave written consent, watched each video individually, wrote responses to free-recall questions, then wrote their identification response on paper. Same 4-video sequence, order counterbalanced.
  • Key controls: Photos all in black & white (no colour cues); target position randomised; video order counterbalanced; instructions explicitly told participants "the person may or may not be there" and pointed to the silhouette for "not present".
  • Closing: Children received crayons and colouring book as a thank-you gift (positive end).
3 Discuss the results of Pozzulo et al.'s study on children's false-positive responding. [8 marks] ▶ Answer
Mark scheme (8 marks): AO1 (4 marks) + AO2 (4 marks).

AO1 — Description of results:
  • All four predictions were supported (Table 3.7).
  • Target-present (identification): Cartoon faces — children 0.99 vs adults 0.95 (χ²=.39, p=.53, no significant difference). Human faces — children 0.23 vs adults 0.66 (χ²=18.83, p=.001, adults significantly better).
  • Target-absent (rejection): Cartoon faces — children 0.74 vs adults 0.94 (χ²=7.66, p=.01, adults significantly better). Human faces — children 0.45 vs adults 0.70 (χ²=5.70, p=.02, adults significantly better).
  • Children's cartoon-ID ≈ adults shows cognitive memory intact for familiar stimuli.
  • Children's rejection rate significantly lower than adults in BOTH cartoon and human conditions.
AO2 — Discussion / Conclusion:
  • The cartoon identification rate of ~99% demonstrates that children's memory for familiar faces is excellent — ruling out cognitive deficits as the cause of errors.
  • Lower target-absent rejection rate (74% vs 94%) suggests errors stem from social factors: the social demand of choosing someone from a line-up ("must pick one").
  • Similarly, human-face conditions show adults outperforming children on both identification and rejection → individual cognitive maturity also matters for unfamiliar faces (likely FFA immaturity in children).
  • Conclusion: errors are relative, not absolute — both cognitive and social factors contribute; but social factors play a larger role in children's target-absent decisions.
4 Outline two strengths of the methodology used in Pozzulo et al.'s study. [4 marks] ▶ Answer
Mark scheme (4 marks): 1 mark per strength with brief description + 1 mark for elaboration. Two strengths required.

Strength 1 — Standardised laboratory procedure: The procedure was highly controlled, with all participants tested under identical conditions. Elaboration: This minimised extraneous variables (e.g., lighting, line-up presentation), increasing internal validity and allowing the researchers to confidently attribute differences to age and target type rather than environmental noise.

Strength 2 — Clever inclusion of cartoon and human stimuli: Comparing familiar (cartoon) and unfamiliar (human) faces separated cognitive demand from social pressure. Elaboration: This is the study's unique methodological strength — it allows researchers to determine whether children's higher false-positive rate is due to cognitive immaturity (which would affect human faces more) or social pressure (which would affect both equally). The finding that children's cartoon-ID ≈ adults but rejection rate lower in target-absent conditions provides clear evidence for the social explanation.
5 Outline two weaknesses of the methodology used in Pozzulo et al.'s study. [4 marks] ▶ Answer
Mark scheme (4 marks): 1 mark per weakness + 1 mark for elaboration. Two weaknesses required.

Weakness 1 — Low ecological validity: Real eyewitness situations involve emotional stress (e.g., fear, danger, urgency). Elaboration: The laboratory setting was quiet and calm, lacking the emotional intensity of real crimes. Findings may therefore not generalise to high-stress forensic situations where children's error patterns could be very different.

Weakness 2 — Ethnic homogeneity of targets: All human targets and foils were Caucasian faces. Elaboration: Real-world line-ups typically involve more diverse ethnicities. Combined with the cross-race effect (people are less accurate at identifying faces of different ethnicities), this limits generalisability to multicultural populations and the broader legal system.

Weakness 3 (alternative) — Demand characteristics: Children were told the study was about TV shows; adults were told it was about "memory". Elaboration: Adults may have guessed the purpose and over-performed, while children may have picked someone to please the researcher. This could have artificially widened or narrowed the child-adult gap, confounding the results.
6 Discuss how the findings of Pozzulo et al.'s study could be applied to real-life forensic practice. [6 marks] ▶ Answer
Mark scheme (6 marks): AO1 (3 marks — describe application) + AO2 (3 marks — evaluate application).

AO1 — Application:
  • The study identifies that social factors drive child false positives in line-ups.
  • This has led to the development of the elimination line-up (Pozzulo & Lindsay, 1999), where children first select the most similar person and then confirm if it IS the target — reducing the social pressure to "just pick one".
  • Studies (Dempsey & Pozzulo, 2008) show child identification can be as reliable as adults when appropriate procedures are used.
AO2 — Evaluation:
  • Positive: The findings have practical value for the legal system — child witnesses are common in abuse/domestic violence cases, and improved procedures reduce miscarriages of justice.
  • Limitation: The original study used low-stress laboratory conditions; real eyewitness situations are highly emotional. Children under stress may exhibit different patterns (e.g., greater reliance on authority figures, or memory collapse).
  • Limitation: The study identifies the problem (social pressure) but the elimination line-up requires further validation in real forensic contexts before universal adoption.
  • Wider issue: The study highlights cross-race effects but doesn't solve them — ethnic diversity in line-ups remains a concern.
7 Suggest two ethical issues raised by Pozzulo et al.'s study and explain how each was addressed. [6 marks] ▶ Answer
Mark scheme (6 marks): 1 mark per issue + 1 mark for explanation + 1 mark for how addressed. Two issues required.

Issue 1 — Informed consent with child participants: Children aged 4–7 cannot legally sign consent forms. How addressed: The researchers used a dual consent procedure — parents/guardians gave written consent, and children gave verbal assent in age-appropriate language. Researchers also explained the child could change their mind at any time without getting in trouble.

Issue 2 — Protection from harm / child wellbeing: Testing young children can cause anxiety, especially around a "memory" task. How addressed: The stimuli were non-crime-related (cartoons + everyday actions like brushing hair) — avoiding violent or traumatic content. A craft activity was conducted before testing to build rapport and reduce stranger-anxiety; ongoing monitoring of fatigue, anxiety, and stress signals was performed; and children received crayons and a colouring book as a positive end to the session.

Issue 3 (alternative) — Right to withdraw: Children may feel unable to refuse adult authority. How addressed: The experimenter explicitly told children "you can change your mind at any time and won't get in trouble", and the experimenters wore smart-casual clothing (not formal/authority-figure uniforms) to reduce authority pressure.
8 Discuss the debate between nature and nurture in relation to Pozzulo et al.'s findings. [6 marks] ▶ Answer
Mark scheme (6 marks): AO1 (3 marks — explain both sides) + AO2 (3 marks — judgement).

AO1 — Both sides:
  • Nature arguments: 4–7-year-olds' prefrontal cortex is immature — weaker inhibitory control makes it physiologically harder to resist "pick someone" impulses. Fusiform face area (FFA) immaturity explains children's poor unfamiliar-face recognition (23% vs 66%). False positive rates may decline naturally with neural maturation.
  • Nurture arguments: Children learn social rules ("answer adults", "be polite") — these are not innate. The cartoon-condition evidence (children's ID ≈100%) rules out cognitive deficits; errors are decision-making, not memory, errors. Different cultures socialise children differently toward authority — collectivist cultures may produce even higher false-positive rates (untested).
AO2 — Judgement:
  • Pozzulo et al.'s findings provide stronger support for nurture: cartoon memory was perfect (99%), so cognitive/innate deficits are ruled out as the cause of rejection errors.
  • However, the human-face results (23% vs 66%) do show individual cognitive maturity effects — supporting nature's role.
  • The most defensible conclusion is that nature and nurture interact: neurodevelopmental level (nature) sets the susceptibility, while socialisation (nurture) determines how that susceptibility is expressed in line-up contexts.
9 Explain what is meant by the ceiling effect in the context of Pozzulo et al.'s study. [2 marks] ▶ Answer
Mark scheme (2 marks): 1 mark for definition + 1 mark for application.

Model answer: A ceiling effect occurs when performance approaches the maximum possible score, preventing further differentiation between groups. In Pozzulo et al.'s study, children's identification rate for cartoon faces was ~99%, meaning there was little room for either group to perform better — both reached near-perfect scores. This is why the researchers used cartoon faces: it controls for cognitive demand (memory is not the limiting factor), isolating the social factor for analysis.
10 Outline what is meant by the individual and situational explanations for behaviour, with reference to Pozzulo et al.'s findings. [6 marks] ▶ Answer
Mark scheme (6 marks): AO1 (3 marks) + AO2 (3 marks).

AO1:
  • The individual explanation suggests behaviour is caused by innate personal characteristics (e.g., personality, biological maturity, cognitive development).
  • The situational explanation suggests behaviour is caused by environmental and social context factors (e.g., authority pressure, social expectations, demand characteristics).
  • In Pozzulo et al.'s study: individual explanation would predict children fail to identify both familiar (cartoon) and unfamiliar (human) faces equally — due to immature cognitive development.
  • Situational explanation would predict children identify familiar faces well (they remember Dora & Diego) but fail to correctly reject when target is absent (social pressure to pick someone).
AO2:
  • Pozzulo's results support the situational explanation: cartoon-ID was ~100% (memory intact), but target-absent rejection was only 74% (vs. 94% adults) — exactly the pattern predicted by situational factors.
  • However, the human-face identification gap (23% vs 66%) does suggest some individual cognitive maturity effect for unfamiliar faces — supporting nature's contribution.
  • Overall, both explanations have merit: the situational explanation is the better fit for the cartoon results, but individual cognitive maturity matters for human faces — an interactionist view is most defensible.
