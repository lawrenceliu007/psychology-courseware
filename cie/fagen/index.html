1. Background — Operant Conditioning

What is Operant Conditioning?

Operant conditioning（操作性条件反射） is a core concept of behaviourist psychology, proposed by B.F. Skinner. The basic logic is straightforward: the consequences of behaviour determine whether that behaviour will be repeated.

For example: when you do something with positive consequences (e.g., studying hard and getting a high exam score), you are more likely to do it again. Psychologists call this mechanism shaping behaviour through its consequences.

Key Terms

operant conditioning（操作性条件反射）: the process of learning through the consequences of our actions. If a behaviour is followed by a reward, it is strengthened; if followed by punishment, it decreases.

positive reinforcer（正强化物）: rewards that satisfy biological needs are called primary positive reinforcers（初级正强化物）, such as food and water. Neutral stimuli that become rewarding through pairing with primary reinforcers are called secondary reinforcers（次级正强化物）, such as sound markers, money, or grades.

Two Types of Reinforcement

  • Positive reinforcement（正强化）: presenting a pleasurable stimulus after a behaviour to increase its frequency. E.g., praise, food rewards, "Well done!"
  • Negative reinforcement（负强化）: removing or avoiding an unpleasant stimulus after a behaviour to increase its frequency. E.g., putting on a thick coat stops you feeling cold (cold is the unpleasant stimulus).

Traditional elephant training methods rely on punishment（惩罚） and negative reinforcement — using pain or fear to force elephants to comply. Modern animal management increasingly favours positive reinforcement（正强化） approaches.

2. Why Elephants? — The TB Problem

Why do elephants need trunk wash training?

Diagnosing and treating diseases in captive elephants is an important part of animal welfare. To avoid using traditional punitive methods, Fagen et al. investigated whether SPR (Secondary Positive Reinforcement，次级正强化) training could teach elephants to reliably and voluntarily participate in a procedure called "trunk wash（象鼻冲洗）" — an operation used to maintain elephant health.

What is Trunk Wash?
This is a method of collecting sputum samples from an elephant's nasal cavity, primarily used to detect tuberculosis (TB，结核病). TB is a serious respiratory disease with an 11%–25% infection rate among captive elephant populations in Nepal, and it can be transmitted from elephants to humans (zoonotic transfer，人畜共患病). Regular testing is therefore essential.

SPR had previously been successfully applied to pandas, primates, antelopes, and many other species, but had never been systematically studied and quantitatively documented in elephants. This study aimed to fill that gap.

Key Term

secondary positive reinforcement (SPR) training（次级正强化训练）: a training method where an animal first learns to associate a neutral stimulus (such as a sound marker/whistle) with a primary reinforcer (such as food), then only this secondary reinforcer is used to indicate correct behaviour, followed later by the actual food reward.

3. Aim

The aim of this study was to determine the feasibility of using SPR training to teach free-contact, traditionally trained elephants in Nepal to voluntarily participate in a trunk wash for the purpose of tuberculosis testing.

Research Hypotheses

  • Even traditionally trained elephants without prior SPR experience can learn complex veterinary cooperation behaviours through positive reinforcement
  • Juvenile elephants will learn new behaviours more easily than adult elephants
  • SPR training can achieve reliable performance within a reasonable timeframe (several weeks)

4. Method

Research Method and Design

This was a controlled observation（受控观察） study involving a small group of captive elephants who received several weeks of training. Researchers observed the elephants' behavioural responses to specific stimuli and used a behavioural checklist（行为核查表） to record responses as percentages. Therefore, this study can also be described as a structured observation（结构化观察）.

Sample

The study included five female elephants: four juveniles and one adult, all housed at the same elephant stable in Nepal.

The elephants were selected because they were docile, not pregnant or nursing calves, and their mahouts（驯象师） were willing to participate. All elephants had been traditionally trained and maintained free contact with their mahouts. No elephant had any previous SPR training experience.

5. Procedure — SPR Training Process

Training Tools: The SPR Trio

The training method in this study was entirely based on SPR technology:

  • Primary Reinforcer（初级强化物）: chopped banana（切碎的香蕉）
  • Secondary Reinforcer（次级强化物/标记）: short whistle blow（短促哨声）
  • Three core techniques: Capture（捕捉）, Lure（诱导）, Shaping（塑形）

The Three Training Techniques

Capture（捕捉法）

Wait for the animal to naturally exhibit the target behaviour, then immediately mark and reward it. Suitable for behaviours the animal spontaneously performs.

E.g.: whistle + banana when the elephant naturally exhales

Lure（诱导法）

Place the reward in a specific position to guide the animal into the desired body posture. Suitable for unnatural body positions.

E.g.: place banana in palm to entice elephant to extend trunk

Shaping（塑形法）

Building on capture or lure, gradually raise the standard — only reward responses that increasingly approximate the target behaviour, "sculpting" the complete behaviour step by step like carving a statue.

E.g.: reward any trunk lift → then only waist height → finally fully upright

Five Behavioural Tasks

Training Phases

Phase 1: Building the Bridge

Repeatedly pair whistle + banana so the elephant learns: whistle = food coming soon. Establishes the conditioned reinforcer（条件强化物）.

Phase 2: Individual Task Training

Use Capture/Lure/Shaping to train each of the five basic tasks. Each elephant learns at their own pace; no preset time limits.

Phase 3: Behaviour Chaining（行为串联）

behaviour chaining: enabling separately trained behaviours to be executed in sequence. Once a behaviour is firmly associated with a primary reinforcer, that behaviour itself becomes the reinforcer for the preceding behaviour.

Trunk Here → Short Steady → Trunk Up → Long Steady → Bucket → Blow

Phase 4: Desensitisation to Syringe

Gradual exposure: seeing syringe from distance → touching outer side of trunk → touching inside nostril → inserting → injecting liquid (starting at 1ml, increasing to 60ml). One elephant who preferred saline over water was switched to plain water, dramatically improving success rate.

Control Measures

  • Verbal cues: single-syllable nonsense words (neither English nor Nepali) — prevents mahout language confusion
  • Mahouts present but silent: no signalling during sessions — controls extraneous variables
  • Voluntary participation: elephants could turn away to refuse — proves behaviour is voluntary
  • Anti-drinking strategy: fresh water before each session

Performance Tests

  • From Session 10 onwards, formal testing approximately every 5 sessions
  • Each test presented 8–16 offers（提示） per behaviour
  • Pass criterion = 80% (8+ correct out of 10 attempts)
  • Once full sequence ≥80%, training considered complete

Why Did Elephant 5 Fail?

  1. Severe interference: neighbouring stall's calf frequently intruded, disrupting attention
  2. Health issues: foot abscess developed during late training, causing irritability
  3. Age factor: ~50 years old, possible vision impairment and reduced trunk strength
  4. Lack of peer reference: trainer could apply insights from juveniles to other juveniles, but not to sole adult

7. Conclusion

  1. SPR is effective for juvenile elephants: four juvenile, free-contact, traditionally trained elephants learned the trunk wash within 35 sessions or fewer
  2. Training is efficient: mean session length ~12 minutes; total training completable within about a week of real time
  3. Behaviour is reliable and voluntary: elephants participated willingly without punishment, producing consistent results
  4. High practical value: SPR can improve health monitoring, trainer-elephant relationships, and animal welfare worldwide
Core conclusion: It is feasible to efficiently train juvenile, free-contact, traditionally trained elephants to voluntarily and reliably participate in a trunk wash using only SPR techniques.

8. Strengths & Weaknesses

Strengths

1. High Ecological Validity

Study took place at a real working elephant stable, not a laboratory. Elephants were in their natural daily environment, making findings realistic and generalisable to similar settings.

2. Quantitative Data Collection

As structured observation: collected quantitative data — minutes per session, number of offers per behaviour, success rates per test. Easier to analyse and compare than unstructured qualitative data.

3. Good Control of Extraneous Variables

  • Mahouts did not intervene — present but silent, excluding traditional training signal interference
  • Nonsense verbal cues — excluded possibility of elephants understanding human language
  • Voluntary participation — elephants could refuse, proving behaviour stemmed from SPR not coercion

4. High Practical / Application Value

Directly served animal welfare goals — improving TB detection while reducing harm to elephants. Not "ivory tower" research but clear real-world application.

5. Innovative Application of Behaviour Chaining

First systematic documentation of behaviour chaining effects in elephants, providing methodological reference for future research.

Weaknesses

1. Very Small Sample (n = 5)

Only 5 elephants (4 juvenile, 1 adult), all female, from the same single stable in Nepal. Difficult to generalise to broader elephant populations.

2. No Control Group

No comparison group using traditional punitive training methods. Cannot quantify how much better SPR is than traditional approaches.

3. Subjective Assessment Criteria

Pass/fail determined subjectively by the trainer (first author) — whether behaviour was "of sufficient quality." No third-party assessment or video review scoring may affect reliability and objectivity.

ER Warning: Fagen used operant conditioning + rewards (NOT punishment). A common student error is saying "Fagen used punishment" — this is factually wrong and will receive zero credit.

4. Unreasonable Steady Test Criteria

Steady task required passing at three positions (up/down/here), but trunk wash only uses up and here positions. Most elephants were already reliable at the two practically relevant positions — test data doesn't reflect real ability.

5. Many Extraneous Variables

Tourist spectators, other animal noise, neighbouring calf intrusion. Flexible individual training plans optimised learning but meant low standardisation, affecting replicability.

6. Gender Limitation

All participants were female; results may not generalise to male elephants.

ER Note on "Training time as weakness": Some students cite "short training time reduced validity" as a weakness. The ER indicates this concern is "minimal" — training duration was sufficient for 4/5 elephants to reach criterion. Not a strong weakness point.

9. Ethical Issues

Ethical Strengths

  1. Used captive rather than wild animals — subjects already in captivity, not wild-caught
  2. No punishment used at any point — exclusively harm-minimising positive reinforcement
  3. Animal welfare prioritised — elephants in good health, no injuries; most time spent freely grazing
  4. Voluntary participation — elephants could choose not to participate (turn away or leave)
  5. Beneficial purpose — aimed at improving elephant herd health management (TB diagnosis/treatment)

Ethical Considerations

  1. Leg chains: elephants leg-chained for much of day, mobile only within limited range. Local standard practice but raises freedom concerns
  2. Desensitisation discomfort: syringe process may have caused some discomfort (though gradual + paired with rewards)
  3. : with n=5, each elephant's data highly influential

10. Issues & Debates

10.1 Animals as Subjects in Psychological Research

Fagen's core premise: elephants can learn complex behavioural sequences through operant conditioning. Skinner's theory originally established through pigeon/rat experiments — Fagen demonstrated cross-species generalisability to highly intelligent elephants.

10.2 Nature vs Nurture

Nature Arguments

  1. Elephants naturally possess excellent memory and learning ability
  2. Trunk has 40,000+ muscles — physiological structure determines fine motor capacity
  3. Elephant 5 (adult) may have failed due to age-related cognitive decline
  4. Individual learning speed differences may reflect innate temperament

Nurture Arguments

  1. All five elephants learned every behaviour entirely through SPR — nurture alone shaped complex behaviour
  2. 4 juveniles succeeded vs 1 adult failed — age/experience (nurture) mattered more than innate factors
  3. Traditional punishment vs SPR produced radically different outcomes — environment determines behaviour
  4. Elephant 5's failure due to environmental disruption (calf intrusion) and health issues, not innate inability

Fagen's Position: Supports Nurture

  • Even elephants with zero SPR experience and years of punitive training can learn through purely positive reinforcement
  • Key factor is training method and individual environment, not innate determinism
  • However: innate trunk flexibility and cognitive ability were prerequisites — without biological foundation, even best training fails

10.3 Animal Welfare vs Scientific Research

PerspectiveViewpoint
Welfare advocatesSPR replaces punitive methods, proving "gentle" is more effective than coercive
Method criticsLeg-chained working elephants; "free contact" lacks safety barrier; true standard should be protected contact
Science defendersWithout this research, thousands of working elephants worldwide would continue suffering under punitive methods

11. Summary

Fagen et al. used five female elephants (4 juvenile, 1 adult) to demonstrate that a trunk wash can be learned through positive reinforcement training. Four out of five elephants successfully learned the procedure within 25–35 training sessions. This was a controlled observation study; the small sample limits generalisability. However, objective quantitative measurements show juvenile elephants can reliably learn complex veterinary cooperation behaviours using SPR.

12. Exam Practice Questions

Q4. What was the purpose of teaching elephants the trunk washing task?
Click to expand/collapse ▼

Key points: To detect tuberculosis (TB). TB has 11%–25% infection rate among captive elephants; sputum samples via trunk wash enable bacterial culture diagnosis. Additionally aimed to prove SPR could replace traditional punitive training for veterinary cooperation.

Exam Tip: Don't just say "to wash their trunk" — that's a result, not a conclusion. The purpose is TB detection + demonstrating SPR feasibility.

Q5. The elephants' mahouts were present but did not speak to or signal the elephants. Explain why.
Click to expand/collapse ▼

Key points: To control extraneous variables. Mahouts normally use traditional signals (verbal/physical). If they continued signalling, impossible to determine whether elephants responded to SPR or to habitual mahout signals. Their neutrality ensured internal validity — confirming it was genuinely whistle + banana SPR at work.

Q6. What influence did individual differences between the elephants have on the results?
Click to expand/collapse ▼

Key points: (1) Age: all 4 juveniles succeeded, only adult failed — age/learning speed key factor; (2) Learning speed variation: Elephant 2 passed in 25 sessions vs others needing 35; (3) Environmental impact: Elephant 5 affected by calf intrusion + foot abscess; (4) These differences both limit generalisability AND suggest SPR suits younger animals better.

Q7. Explain what is meant by 'shaping' in this study. Give an example.
Click to expand/collapse ▼

Key points: Shaping = gradually raising standards, only rewarding responses closest to target each time. Example: Training "Trunk Up" — reward any slight lift → then only waist height → then head height → finally fully vertical. Each step demands slightly more; elephant approaches target through trial and error.

ER Tip: Must give a concrete example FROM THE STUDY, not a generic definition. "Rewarding progressively closer approximations" alone won't get full marks.

Q8. Describe one strength and one weakness of this study.
Click to expand/collapse ▼

Strength examples: Quantitative data (objective, analysable) OR high ecological validity (real setting) OR good control of variables (mahouts neutral, nonsense cues).

Weakness examples: Very small sample (n=5, poor generalisability) OR no control group OR subjective pass/fail criteria (trainer-judged, affects reliability).

Avoid: Saying "used punishment" (factually wrong — Fagen used ONLY positive reinforcement). ER explicitly marks this as zero-credit error.

Q9. Discuss whether this research supports a nature or nurture view of animal behaviour.
Click to expand/collapse ▼

Position: Supports nurture.

Evidence: (1) All behaviours learned through SPR — no elephant innately knew trunk wash; (2) Different methods (punishment vs SPR reward) produced different outcomes — environment determines behaviour; (3) Age/environmental factors predicted success better than innate factors.

Nuance: Nature provided prerequisites (trunk flexibility, cognitive capacity) — but nurture determined whether learning occurred. Best answers acknowledge both while taking a clear position.
