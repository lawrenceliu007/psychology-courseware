1. Background — Operant Conditioning

What is Operant Conditioning?

Operant conditioning（操作性条件反射） is a core concept of behaviourist psychology, proposed by B.F. Skinner. The basic logic is straightforward: the consequences of behaviour determine whether that behaviour will be repeated.

For example: when you do something with positive consequences (e.g., studying hard and getting a high exam score), you are more likely to do it again. Psychologists call this mechanism shaping behaviour through its consequences.

🔑 Key Words

operant conditioning（操作性条件反射）: the process of learning through the consequences of our actions. If a behaviour is followed by a reward, it is strengthened; if followed by punishment, it decreases.

positive reinforcer（正强化物）: rewards that satisfy biological needs are called primary positive reinforcers（初级正强化物）, such as food and water. Neutral stimuli that become rewarding through pairing with primary reinforcers are called secondary reinforcers（次级正强化物）, such as sound markers, money, or grades.

Two Types of Reinforcement

  • Positive reinforcement（正强化）: presenting a pleasurable stimulus after a behaviour to increase its frequency. E.g., praise, food rewards, "Well done!"
  • Negative reinforcement（负强化）: removing or avoiding an unpleasant stimulus after a behaviour to increase its frequency. E.g., putting on a thick coat stops you feeling cold (cold is the unpleasant stimulus), so you are more willing to wear one.

Traditional elephant training methods rely on punishment（惩罚） and negative reinforcement — using pain or fear to force elephants to comply. Modern animal management increasingly favours positive reinforcement（正强化） approaches.

🔑 Key Words

captive animal welfare（圈养动物福利）: when psychologists work with captive or confined animals, they must ensure the animals' health and wellbeing are safeguarded — including appropriate housing, space, food, water, and care.

positive reinforcement（正强化）: a form of operant conditioning where desirable behaviours are encouraged by providing rewards. E.g., praising a child for saying "please" and "thank you" positively reinforces polite behaviour.

2. Why Elephants?

Why do elephants need training?

Diagnosing and treating diseases in captive elephants is an important part of animal welfare. To avoid using traditional punitive methods, Fagen et al. investigated whether SPR (Secondary Positive Reinforcement，次级正强化) training could teach elephants to reliably and voluntarily participate in a procedure called "trunk wash（象鼻冲洗）" — an operation used to maintain elephant health.

💡 What is Trunk Wash（象鼻冲洗）?
This is a method of collecting sputum samples from an elephant's nasal cavity, primarily used to detect tuberculosis (TB，结核病). TB is a serious respiratory disease with an 11%–25% infection rate among captive elephant populations, and it can be transmitted from elephants to humans (zoonotic transfer，人畜共患病). Regular testing is therefore essential.

SPR had previously been successfully applied to pandas, primates, antelopes, and many other species, but had never been systematically studied and quantitatively documented in elephants. This study aimed to fill that gap.

🔑 Key Word

secondary positive reinforcement (SPR) training（次级正强化训练）: a training method where an animal first learns to associate a neutral stimulus (such as a sound marker/whistle) with a primary reinforcer (such as food), then only this secondary reinforcer is used to indicate correct behaviour, followed later by the actual food reward.

3. Aim

The aim of this study was to see whether free-contact, traditionally trained elephants can be trained to participate in a trunk wash by using positive reinforcement.

Implicit Research Hypotheses（隐含假设）

  • Even traditionally trained elephants without prior SPR experience can learn complex veterinary cooperation behaviours through positive reinforcement
  • Juvenile elephants will learn new behaviours more easily than adult elephants
  • SPR training can achieve reliable performance within a reasonable timeframe (several weeks)

4. Method

Research Method and Design

This was a controlled observation（受控观察） study involving a small group of captive elephants who received several weeks of training. Researchers observed the elephants' behavioural responses to specific stimuli and used a behavioural checklist（行为核查表） to record responses as percentages. Therefore, this study can also be described as a structured observation（结构化观察）.

Sample

The study included five female elephants: four juveniles and one adult, all housed at the same elephant stable in Nepal.

5. Procedure — SPR Training Process

Training Tools: The SPR Trio

The training method in this study was entirely based on SPR technology:

  • Primary Reinforcer（初级强化物）: chopped banana（切碎的香蕉）
  • Secondary Reinforcer（次级强化物/标记）: short whistle blow（短促哨声）
  • Three core techniques: Capture（捕捉）, Lure（诱导）, Shaping（塑形）
SPR Classical Conditioning（SPR 经典条件反射）— Three stages: (1) Bananas (UCS) → Happy elephant (UCR); (2) Whistle (NS) + Bananas (UCS) → Happy elephant; (3) Whistle alone (CS) → Happy elephant (CR)

The Three Training Techniques Explained

🎯 Capture（捕捉法）

Wait for the animal to naturally exhibit the target behaviour, then immediately mark and reward it. Suitable for behaviours the animal spontaneously performs.

E.g.: immediately whistle + give banana when the elephant naturally exhales

🍌 Lure（诱导法）

Place the reward in a specific position to guide the animal into the desired body posture. Suitable for unnatural body positions.

E.g.: place banana in palm to entice elephant to extend trunk towards hand

✨ Shaping（塑形法）

Building on capture or lure, gradually raise the standard — only reward responses that increasingly approximate the target behaviour, "sculpting" the complete behaviour step by step like carving a statue. This is the most refined and powerful technique.

E.g.: first reward any trunk-lifting motion → then only reward lifting to waist height → finally only reward fully upright posture

Training Process in Detail

Phase 1: Building the Bridge（建立标记-奖励联结）

First, repeatedly pair the whistle + banana so the elephant learns: hearing the whistle = food coming soon. This establishes the conditioned reinforcer（条件强化物）.

Phase 2: Training Each Behaviour Individually

Use Capture/Lure/Shaping to train each of the five basic tasks above. Each elephant learns at their own pace; no preset time limits — everything depends on individual mastery.

Phase 3: Behaviour Chaining（行为串联）

🔑 Key Word

behaviour chaining（行为串联）: the process of enabling separately trained behaviours to be executed in sequence. The theory behind it: once a behaviour is firmly associated with a primary reinforcer, that behaviour itself becomes the reinforcer for the preceding behaviour — completing the first behaviour earns the "right" to perform the second.

Specific approach: first pair bucket + blow → then add various combinations of other behaviours → finally chain the full sequence:

Trunk Here → Short Steady → Trunk Up → Long Steady → Bucket → Blow ✅

Phase 4: Desensitisation to Syringe（注射器脱敏）

🔑 Key Word

desensitisation（脱敏）: gradually reducing and eliminating a response to a stimulus (e.g., fear of syringes). Through gradual exposure — seeing syringe from distance → touching outer side of trunk tip → touching inside nostril → inserting into nostril → injecting small amounts of liquid (starting at 1ml, gradually increasing to 60ml) — the elephant gradually adapts.

Key Control Measures（关键控制措施）

  • Verbal cues（语言提示词）: single-syllable, nonsense made-up words (neither English nor Nepali), preventing mahouts from assuming elephants understand human language
  • Mahouts do not intervene: mahouts present but silent and non-signalling, ensuring training purity
  • Voluntary participation: elephants could turn away or leave to refuse — no coercion
  • Anti-drinking strategy: fresh water provided before training to prevent elephants drinking the saline sample; one elephant who preferred saltwater was switched to water, dramatically improving success rate

Performance Tests（测试方式）

  • From session 10 onwards, formal testing approximately every 5 sessions
  • Each test presented 8–16 offers（提示/指令） per behaviour
  • Pass criterion = 80% (8 or more correct out of 10 attempts)
  • Once full sequence ≥80%, training considered complete

"Trunk Here" was the hardest task — it required the elephant to overcome instinctive distrust and actively place its most sensitive body part (the trunk tip) in a stranger's hand. This explains why it needed nearly 300 offers to pass.

Why Did Elephant 5 Fail?

The original paper discussed multiple possible factors:

  1. Severe interference: a neighbouring stall's calf frequently intruded into the training area, disrupting attention
  2. Health issues: a foot abscess（足部脓肿）developed during late training, causing irritability and poor focus
  3. Age factor: approximately 50 years old, possible vision impairment and reduced trunk strength
  4. Lack of peer reference: the other four were all juveniles; the trainer could rapidly apply insights from one juvenile to others, but could not do this for the sole adult

7. Conclusion

The researchers drew the following conclusions:

  1. SPR is effective for juvenile elephants: four juvenile, free-contact, traditionally trained elephants learned the trunk wash within 35 sessions or fewer
  2. Training is efficient: mean session length only ~12 minutes; total training completable within about a week
  3. Behaviour is reliable: elephants' responses were voluntary（自愿的）, producing reliable results without punishment
  4. High generalisability potential: SPR can be applied to captive elephant management programmes worldwide, improving health monitoring, trainer-elephant relationships, and animal welfare
Core conclusion: This study proves that it is feasible to efficiently train juvenile, free-contact, traditionally trained elephants to voluntarily participate in a trunk wash using only SPR training techniques.

8. Strengths & Weaknesses

✅ Strengths

1. High Ecological Validity（高生态效度）

The study took place at a real working elephant stable, not a laboratory setting. Elephants were in their natural daily environment, making findings more realistic and generalisable.

2. Quantitative Data Collection（定量数据收集）

As a structured observation, the study collected quantitative data — minutes per session, number of offers per behaviour, success rates per test. Quantitative data is easier to analyse and compare than unstructured observation data.

3. Good Control of Variables（控制变量充分）

  • Mahouts did not intervene: mahouts present but not signalling, excluding interference from traditional training signals
  • Nonsense verbal cues: made-up words excluded the possibility of elephants understanding human language
  • Voluntary participation: elephants could refuse, proving behaviour genuinely stemmed from positive reinforcement rather than coercion

4. High Practical Value（实践价值高）

The research directly served animal welfare goals — improving TB detection methods while reducing harm to elephants. This was not an "ivory tower" study but one with clear practical application value.

5. Innovative Application of Behaviour Chaining

This was the first systematic documentation of behaviour chaining effects in elephants, providing methodological reference for future research.

❌ Weaknesses

1. Very Small Sample（样本量极小）

Only 5 elephants (4 juvenile, 1 adult), all from the same stable. Difficult to generalise to the elephant population. Can they represent all captive elephants? Hard to say.

2. No Control Group（缺乏对照组）

No control group using traditional punitive training methods for comparison. So while we know SPR works, we cannot quantify how much better it is than traditional methods.

3. Subjective Assessment Criteria（主观评分标准）

The pass/fail criterion was determined subjectively by the trainer (first author) — whether behaviour was "good enough for use in actual trunk washing." No third-party assessment and no video review scoring may affect reliability（信度） and objectivity（客观性）.

4. Unreasonable Steady Task Test Criteria

The Steady task pass criterion required meeting standards at three positions (up/down/here), but actual trunk wash only requires steady at up and here positions. This means Steady task test data cannot accurately reflect elephants' real ability — most elephants were already reliable at the two practically relevant positions.

5. Many Extraneous Variables（外部干扰因素多）

Tourist spectators, other animal noise, neighbouring calf intrusion and other distractions may have affected elephants' focus and willingness to participate. Meanwhile, each elephant's training plan was flexibly adjusted for individual differences — this flexibility optimised learning outcomes but also meant low standardisation（标准化程度低）, affecting replicability（可复制性）.

6. Gender Limitation（性别局限）

All participants were female; results may not apply to male elephants.

9. Ethical Issues

✅ Ethical Strengths

  1. Used captive rather than wild animals: subjects were already-captive elephants, not wild-caught animals. A more ethically sound choice.
  2. Avoided punishment: no punitive methods were used at any point, focusing exclusively on harm-minimising positive reinforcement.
  3. Animal welfare prioritised: elephants reported in good health with no physical injuries. They spent most of the day freely grazing in the jungle, able to move and socialise with other elephants.
  4. Voluntary participation: elephants could choose not to participate (turn away or leave); no coercion.
  5. Beneficial purpose: the research aimed to develop useful methods for elephant herd health management — helping rapid diagnosis and treatment of TB, highly beneficial to the elephants themselves.

⚠️ Ethical Considerations

  1. Leg chains（腿链限制）: elephants were leg-chained in stalls for much of the day, mobile only within a limited range. This is local standard practice but raises ethical questions about animal freedom.
  2. Discomfort during desensitisation: the syringe desensitisation process may have caused some discomfort (though gradual and paired with rewards).
  3. Small sample limitations: with only 5 elephants, statistical power was limited — meaning each elephant's data was highly "valuable," but also that individual anomalies could disproportionately affect overall results.

10. Issues & Debates

10.1 Can Animals Serve as Subjects in Psychological Research?

Fagen's core premise: elephants can learn complex behavioural sequences through operant conditioning. This supports a broader argument — humans and animals share fundamental learning mechanisms（学习机制）.

Skinner's operant conditioning theory was originally established through pigeon and rat experiments. Fagen successfully applied the same principles to elephants — a species with far greater intelligence than rodents — demonstrating the theory's cross-species generalisability（跨物种普适性）.

However, this also sparks debate:

  • Supporters: animal research allows us to study basic learning processes under controlled conditions, free from human language, culture, and social factors. Elephant research directly improves animal welfare.
  • Critics: elephants are highly social and cognitively complex animals. Is treating them merely as "learning machines" ignoring their inner experience? Does leg-chained captivity itself violate animal rights?

10.2 Nature vs Nurture: How Much of Elephant Behaviour Is "Innate"?

🧬 Nature Arguments（先天论点）

  1. Elephants naturally possess excellent memory and learning ability — wild elephants need no training to remember water sources and social relationships
  2. The trunk itself is a highly flexible organ with over 40,000 muscles — physiological structure（生理结构） determines their capacity for fine motor operations
  3. Elephant 5 (adult) may have been unable to learn due to age-related cognitive decline（认知衰退） — suggesting biological limits on learning ability
  4. Individual differences in learning speed (25 vs 35 sessions) may reflect differences in innate personality/temperament（先天性格/气质）

🌱 Nurture Arguments（后天论点）

  1. All five elephants learned every behavioural component entirely through SPR — proving nurture alone can shape complex behaviour
  2. Four juveniles' success vs one adult's failure strongly suggests age/past experience（nurture factors） matter more than innate factors
  3. Traditional training (punishment-based) vs SPR (reward-based) produced radically different outcomes — demonstrating that training method (environmental factor) determines behavioural output
  4. Elephant 5's failure was primarily due to environmental disruption (calf intrusion) and health issues (foot abscess), not innate inability
🎯 Fagen's position: This study clearly supports a Nurture（后天） argument. Even elephants with zero SPR experience and years of traditional punitive training can learn complex behaviours through purely positive reinforcement methods. The key lies in training method and individual environment, not innate determinism.

10.3 Balancing Animal Welfare and Scientific Research

Fagen's study sits at an interesting intersection:

Perspective（角度）Viewpoint（观点）
Animal welfare advocatesThis research itself serves animal welfare — SPR replaces punitive methods, directly improving elephants' quality of life. It proves that being "gentle" with elephants is not just morally right but actually more effective.
Method criticsSubjects remain leg-chained working elephants. "Free contact" essentially means no safety barrier between trainer and elephant — if an elephant suddenly becomes aggressive, both parties could be injured. True highest-welfare standard should be "protected contact（有屏障保护接触）" systems.
Scientific value defendersWithout such research, we would never know whether SPR works for elephants. Knowing this helps thousands of working elephants worldwide receive better treatment. Short-term research participation trades off against long-term welfare gains.

10.4 Special Significance: Providing Evidence for the "Impossible"

Before Fagen's study, many mahouts likely believed "elephants could never voluntarily cooperate with a trunk wash." This study provided hard data（硬数据） proving this "impossible" was actually possible — given the right method.

This parallels Bandura's Bobo Doll experiment: both demonstrate the power of observational/reinforcement learning exceeding intuitive expectations. Bandura showed children can learn aggression through observation; Fagen showed elephants can learn complex veterinary cooperation through positive reinforcement. Together they support the Learning Approach's core proposition: behaviour is largely learned, not fixed.

11. Summary

Fagen et al. used a group of five female elephants (4 juvenile, 1 adult) to demonstrate that a trunk wash can be learned through positive reinforcement training. Through extensive training and testing, researchers confirmed that most animals could execute a series of actions designed for health examination. This was a controlled observation（受控观察） study; the small sample size limits its generalisability and may make replication difficult. However, using objective quantitative measurements (percentage of correct responses) does show that juvenile elephants can learn the trunk wash using SPR training.

12. Exam Practice Questions

Q4. What was the purpose of teaching elephants the trunk washing task?

Key points: To detect tuberculosis (TB). TB is a serious respiratory disease in captive elephants (11%–25% infection rate); sputum samples collected via trunk wash enable bacterial culture diagnosis. Additionally, the study aimed to prove SPR methods could replace traditional punitive training for elephant veterinary cooperation.

Q5. The elephants' mahouts were present in each session. However, they didn't look at the elephants or speak to them. Can you explain why the mahouts were asked to behave this way?

Key points: To control extraneous variables（额外变量）. Mahouts normally use traditional methods (including verbal commands and physical signals) to train elephants. If they continued giving these signals while present, it would be impossible to determine whether elephants were responding to SPR training or to the mahouts' habitual signals. Their neutrality ensured the study's internal validity（内部效度） — confirming it was genuinely the whistle + banana SPR at work.

Q6. What influence do you think individual differences between the elephants had on the results of this study?

Key points: (1) Age difference: all 4 juveniles succeeded, only adult failed — age/learning speed may be a key factor; (2) Learning speed variation: Elephant 2 passed in 25 sessions vs others needing 35 — individual temperament/cognitive ability differs; (3) Environmental impact: Elephant 5 affected by neighbouring calf intrusion and own health issues (foot abscess); (4) These differences are both limitations (reduce generalisability) and interesting findings (SPR may suit younger animals better).

Q7. Explain what is meant by 'shaping' in the context of this study. Give an example from the elephant training.

Key points: Shaping is a training method that gradually raises standards — only rewarding responses closest to the target each time, "sculpting" the complete behaviour step by step. For example, when training "Trunk Up": first reward any slight trunk lift → then only reward lifting to waist height → then only reward lifting to head height → finally only reward fully vertical posture. Each step demands slightly more, and the elephant gradually approaches the target through trial and error.

Q8. Describe one strength and one weakness of this study.

Key points:
Strength example: Use of quantitative data (percentages, session counts, durations) makes results objective and analysable; OR: High ecological validity (real environment, real elephants); OR: Good control of variables (mahouts neutral, nonsense verbal cues).
Weakness example: Very small sample (n=5) from single location, poor generalisability; OR: No control group for comparison with traditional methods; OR: Pass criteria subjectively judged by trainer, lacking third-party assessment affecting reliability.

Q9. Discuss whether this research supports a nature or nurture view of animal behaviour.

Key points: The study clearly supports a nurture（后天） view. Evidence: (1) All behaviours were learned through SPR training — no elephant innately knew how to do a trunk wash; (2) Different methods (traditional punishment vs SPR reward) produced different outcomes — showing environment/training method determines behaviour; (3) Age/environmental factors (juveniles succeeded, adult failed due to disruptions) predicted learning outcomes better than innate factors. However, nature also played a role — elephants' innate trunk flexibility and cognitive ability were prerequisites（前提条件） for learning (without this innate foundation, even the best training methods would fail).
